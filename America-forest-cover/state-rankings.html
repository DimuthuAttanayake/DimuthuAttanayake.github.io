Land Use · 1945 to 2017

The forests America lost, and found again

In 70 years, the United States traded tens of millions of acres of farmland and forest for cities, parks and ranches. But the trades did not happen everywhere. New York, Alabama and Georgia grew their forests back as old farms were abandoned. California and Texas lost their forests to range and suburb. Watch every state make its own swap, one census at a time.

Each of the 48 contiguous states sits on its own row, ordered by how much its forest cover has grown or shrunk since 1945. As you change the year, the rows resort and the country's winners and losers reorder themselves.

About the forest fields. "All forest" is the USDA's total forest-use land for each state. "Grazed" is forest that doubles as cattle range, mostly in the West. "Ungrazed" is timber and conservation forest with no grazing. "Percent of state" normalizes by state size so heavily forested but small states (Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont) read fairly against larger states. States that started at zero grazed forest in 1945 and stayed there sit at zero on the chart.
