Global Carbon Emissions Are Growing: Here’s What’s Driving It!

By Dimuthu Attanayake

A short-lived pause in global carbon pollution was expected to end in 2017, as emissions climbed again with a resurgence in fossil-fuel use and industrial activity.

The projected increase signaled that recent stability in emissions had been less a turning point than a temporary lull, raising fresh concerns about how quickly global energy demand can outpace gains from cleaner technologies

Global carbon budget data shows that global carbon dioxide emissions were projected to rise by about 2% in 2017

This comes after three years of near-flat growth from 2014 to 2016.

The emissions are expected to reach a record high, of roughly 37 billion tonnes of CO₂.

Carbon emissions,primarily carbon dioxide (CO₂), are greenhouse gases released when fossil fuels like coal, oil and natural gas are burned for energy, as well as through industrial activities and deforestation.

The main challenge is that these emissions are deeply embedded in everyday systems—powering electricity, transportation and industry, making them difficult to reduce quickly. As global demand for energy grows, cutting emissions requires not just cleaner technologies, but a broad shift in how energy is produced and used across economies

More broadly, the rebound highlights how tightly emissions remain linked to economic growth.

Even as renewable energy expands, rising demand for electricity, transport and heavy industry continues to drive fossil-fuel consumption—underscoring the difficulty of achieving sustained declines without deeper structural changes to the global energy system.

Rise in industrial activities in China is the main contributor to this increase in carbon emissions.

In India, emissions grew by 6 percent over the past 3 years, but slowed in 2017.

Brazil and Japan are also showing a reduction in emissions for 2017.

When emissions from burning fossil fuels and industrial activity outpace the amount of carbon absorbed by forests, soil, and oceans, this causes atmospheric CO₂ to rise steadily.

Even with natural sinks at work, more carbon stayed in the atmosphere, trapping heat and accelerating climate change, highlighting the urgent need to cut emissions.

This year‑to‑year increase in carbon dioxide in the air is called " atmospheric growth."

Atmospheric concentrations too are currently on the rise, with the total carbon dioxide emissions.

Other factors, such as El Niño, which gives rise to high temperatures and less carbon absorption, also contribute to this.

Slowing atmospheric growth will require more than protecting forests and oceans—it demands deep, sustained cuts to fossil-fuel emissions.

Without reducing the carbon humans release, natural sinks alone cannot keep up, and CO₂ will continue to accumulate, locking in higher temperatures and intensifying the risks of climate change worldwide.
