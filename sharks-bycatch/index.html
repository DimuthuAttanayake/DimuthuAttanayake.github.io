When Sharks Become Intentional Bycatch

Commercially viable species living in the high seas are the least likely to be released back.

Photo by Valeriia Miller/Pexels

By Dimuthu Attanayake

Commercially viable species of sharks caught as bycatch while fishing remain one of the biggest threats to shark populations globally. Large numbers of these sharks are never returned to the oceans, raising concern that some sharks are being deliberately captured, rather than being accidentally caught. .

When fishers target other commercially demanded species like tuna, sometimes sharks too are unintentionally captured and this process is referred to as bycatch. However, intentional bycatch is often considered an Illegal, Unreported, and Unregulated (IUU) fishing method, with an estimated 50 million sharks ending up as bycatch each year. Many local and regional frameworks demand that these sharks be released back to the ocean, but this is not always the case.

Often, sharks that have commercial value, whether for their meat, fins, or both, are more likely to be retained rather than released, even when caught as bycatch, says Simon McKinley, Spatial Ecologist from the Shark Ecology Project at Charles Darwin Foundation in Galapagos Islands. According to him, this practice extends to protected shark species.

“This is because they can be sold, providing an additional source of income to fishers alongside their target catch, making the fishing trip more worth the time, money and effort,” he says.

Global shark meat trade is a multi billion industry, while the global shark fin trade remains a multi million industry, lucrative despite attempts to regulate shark killings. Shark finning and use of certain fishing gear too is restricted by international legal frameworks such as Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES ).

Spiny dogfish meat for instance, is in high demand in Europe for fish and chips, and Shortfin mako, known as the oceans fastest shark is also prized for its meat, and is kept 75 percent of the time according to this analysis. Back when the International Commission for the Conservation of Atlantic Tunas banned mako shark bycatch retention in 2021, fishers responded by recording more sharks as “dead on arrival”.

As a keystone species, sharks are a vital part of the marine ecosystem, helping to balance out the ecology through predator prey relations.

“..high levels of bycatch can lead to population declines since sharks grow slowly, reach maturity late, and produce relatively few young,” McKinley says.

Once caught by fishers, sharks’ survival also depend on their habitat:

Because sharks share habitats with commercially valuable species, they are caught in many fisheries worldwide, particularly those using non-selective fishing methods such as trawling, purse seining, and longlining. This is a problem because capture can cause death, injury, or stress that reduces the shark’s chances of survival even if it is released."
